What are our Kindles made of?

A few of our group members had kindles as kids, and Dakota still uses one to this day. As younger aspiring engineers, we were curious about what is actually inside of the kindles we were using. Over the course of this project, we satisfied our childish dream of taking apart a Kindle and seeing for ourselves the materials inside of it.

We looked closely at two parts of the Kindle. The first is the transparent electrode that makes the e-ink screen work (figure 1 botom right). The second is the thin stainless-steel cover that sits between the reader and a lithium-ion battery(figure 1 top left). We performed a variety of tests on the chosen products, along with researching the feasibility of recycling each of them.

01 · Kindle 101

Ink you can switch on and off

A Kindle's screen doesn't glow like a phone's. It uses an electrophoretic (e-ink) display. Millions of microcapsules sit between two electrodes, and each capsule holds positively and negatively charged black and white pigment particles in a clear fluid. When a voltage is applied across a capsule, one colour of particle is pulled toward the top of the capsule, so that spot shows black or white.

Power is only needed to switch a capsule. Once the particles have moved they stay put with no power at all, which is why a page of text costs almost nothing to display and a Kindle battery lasts for weeks.

02 · The screen

A conductor you can see through

For the circuit in Figure 2 to work, the top electrode has to conduct electricity while letting you see the ink underneath. The Kindle does this with a thin plastic film coated in indium tin oxide (ITO). The coating is invisible, but it adds a conductive layer that closes the circuit across every capsule.

Is there really indium in it? (XRF)

The usual ITO recipe is about 90% indium oxide and 10% tin oxide by weight, which works out to roughly 74% indium, 8% tin and 18% oxygen. We scanned the coated film twice with the X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analyser, expecting indium to stand out.

No indium appeared. The two scans also disagree with each other, and they look much more like the material underneath the coating than the coating itself. The XRF signal goes far deeper than a coating only nanometres thick, so it mostly measures the substrate. The "scandium" is almost certainly calcium, a known mix-up in our XRF software because the two elements' X-ray peaks overlap. As a check, we scanned a piece of confirmed ITO glass from the materials science lab, and it didn't show indium either. Our conclusion is that this instrument can't detect a coating this thin. It doesn't mean the indium is missing.

Is it conductive? (four-point probe)

To check that the coating conducts, we placed the film on a four-point probe connected to a power supply. Current flowed through the film, which confirms there is a conductive layer on the plastic even though we can't see it.

Structure and bonding: why ITO?

ITO is a ceramic, not a metal. Its base is indium oxide (In2O3), an ionic–covalent compound with a cubic crystal structure called bixbyite. Its usefulness comes from two properties that normally don't go together:

  • Transparency. In2O3 has a wide optical band gap of about 3.5 eV. That is the minimum photon energy needed to push an electron across the gap and absorb the light. Visible-light photons carry only about 1.6–3.1 eV, so they pass straight through.
  • Conductivity. In ITO, some In3+ ions in the crystal are replaced by Sn4+ ions. Each tin ion brings one extra electron that the lattice doesn't need for bonding, and that free electron can carry current. The indium-to-tin ratio is tuned to give enough free electrons to conduct without making the film cloudy.

The element indium has its own story. As a pure metal it forms a body-centred tetragonal crystal held together by fairly weak metallic bonds, with a cohesive energy of about 2.52 eV/atom compared with 3.49 eV/atom for copper. Weak bonds explain why indium is so soft and melts so easily.

Once indium is locked into the oxide ceramic, those metallic properties disappear. It is the oxide's wide band gap and tin's extra electrons that make the screen work.

Identifying the alloy

We clipped a tab off the cover and scanned it with XRF. It is mostly iron, with large amounts of chromium and nickel and a little manganese, which is the signature of an austenitic stainless steel. Comparing our numbers with the alloy data in Ansys Granta EduPack, the closest match is 301 stainless steel.

The chromium is what makes it stainless. It forms a thin, self-healing chromium oxide film on the surface, which gives 301 excellent resistance to water and humidity and moderate resistance to acids and alkalis. That matters for a part sitting on a battery inside a device people carry everywhere.

04 · Use phase

What happens when the battery swells?

In normal use the battery barely pushes on its cover. The worst case is an ageing or damaged lithium-ion cell that swells as gas builds up inside, pressing outward on the middle of the cover while the screws and edges hold it in place.

How hard does a swelling battery push? One study measured the force from swelling lithium-ion cells and found the force rising by roughly 50 N or more above its starting preload, whether that preload was 50 N or 250 N (Figure 11). We used 50 N as a reasonable estimate of what our cover has to resist.

Testing it: three-point bending

We modelled this load as flexure. The cover is supported near its two ends and pushed at the centre, so its outer face stretches (tension) while its inner face squeezes (compression). We reproduced this on an Instron universal testing machine as a three-point bend test.

Maximum bending stress at 50 N (flat-sheet model)

M = WL/4 = (50 N)(0.070 m)/4 = 0.875 N·m
I = wh3/12 = (0.060 m)(0.00025 m)3/12 = 7.81 × 10−14 m4
c = h/2 = 1.25 × 10−4 m
σmax = Mc/I = (0.875)(1.25 × 10−4)/(7.81 × 10−14) ≈ 1.4 × 109 Pa

σmax ≈ 1.4 GPa, nearly twice the 758 MPa yield strength.

If the cover were a flat 0.25 mm sheet, a swelling battery would bend it permanently. By the same equation, a flat sheet would start to yield at only about 27 N.

Our real cover held about 185 N in the Instron before it gave way, roughly seven times more. The difference is the 2.44 mm bent walls around its edges. Like the flanges on an I-beam, they move material away from the neutral axis, which raises the moment of inertia far more than adding thickness would. The designers got a stiff, protective cover out of very little steel by bending its edges up.

05 · End of life

How hard is it to take back apart?

Before the cover can be recycled as clean stainless steel, everything stuck to it has to come off. Here is how our teardown went:

  1. Pry off the Kindle's outer plastic case.
  2. Remove the two screws holding the battery cover to the chassis.
  3. Separate the lithium-ion battery from the cover, which it is bonded to with adhesive.
  4. Peel the black foam pad off the cover. Underneath is a thin plastic film, which also peels off by hand.
  5. Wipe off the leftover adhesive with ethanol.

Peeling foam tape takes up to about 35 N [3], easily done by hand. The real stress state is complicated, with tension in the adhesive and some twisting. To estimate the stress in the steel, we assumed the peel force pulls along the face of the cover, which loads its cross-section in simple tension:

Stress in the cover during peeling

A = w × t = 60.10 mm × 0.25 mm = 15.0 mm²
σ = F/A = 35 N / 15.0 mm² ≈ 2.3 MPa
ε = σ/E = 2.3 MPa / 189 GPa ≈ 1.2 × 10−5 (about 0.001%)

About 0.3% of the yield strength: purely elastic, and the steel springs back unharmed.

Taking the cover apart barely loads the steel at all. And since the steel is going to be melted down, a little bending wouldn't matter anyway.

Our verdict on this part

The battery cover is easy to recycle: two screws and a peel. It would be even easier without the adhesive. The bent walls and screws already hold the battery in place, so the glue mostly adds a cleaning step (and a solvent) between the device and a clean stream of stainless steel.

06 · The bigger picture

A single Kindle is a tiny fraction of that, but there are many millions of them in drawers and landfills. Our two parts show both sides of the recycling story.

The battery cover is the good news. Stainless steel is valuable, easy to identify and widely recycled, and this cover comes out with a screwdriver. The bigger risk in the same compartment is the lithium-ion battery. A damaged or swollen cell is exactly the failure we modelled in Section 04, and crushed batteries are a known cause of fires in waste and recycling facilities [9].

The screen is the harder case. The indium in the ITO film is spread in a layer so thin our XRF couldn't find it. Indium is on the U.S. critical minerals list [5]. It is mined almost entirely as a by-product of zinc refining, and most of the world's refined supply comes from China [5]. A thin film bonded to plastic is about the hardest form to recover a metal from, so most of the indium in a discarded e-reader is simply lost.

The Kindle's most recyclable part is a plain sheet of steel. Its scarcest material is spread nanometres thin across the screen, which is the part that makes it a Kindle at all.
