The Kindle provides a small-scale example of a broader e-waste problem: material value does not necessarily translate into practical recovery. The two components examined in this project illustrate different end-of-life constraints, from separating a recyclable metal cover to recovering a thin conductive coating from a layered display.

Material composition and recovery

The stainless-steel battery cover offers a relatively straightforward recovery pathway once separated from the materials bonded to it. Stainless steel is valuable, identifiable, and widely recycled. The adjacent lithium-ion battery introduces a separate handling constraint. Battery swelling is the failure mechanism modelled on the Battery Cover page, while damage during waste processing can create a fire hazard. The U.S. EPA documents lithium-ion battery fires in waste and recycling facilities [9].

The display presents a more complex separation problem. Our XRF analysis did not detect indium in the ITO film, consistent with the measurement limitations discussed on the E-ink Screen page. Indium is listed as a U.S. critical mineral and is produced primarily as a by-product of zinc refining [5]. Its distribution in a thin coating within a layered component makes recovery a different engineering problem from recycling a discrete metal sheet. Our testing identified this separation challenge but did not measure an indium recovery rate.

Adhesive bonding and repairability

During disassembly, we repeatedly found adhesive or double-sided tape in addition to mechanical fasteners. Removing the screws did not release the components; the remaining adhesive bonds still resisted separation. This observation raised a design question: what additional function did the adhesive provide where screws already appeared to secure the assembly?

The screen demonstrated the clearest consequence of this construction. We could not remove it intact and instead had to fracture it progressively, removing it piece by piece. The removal process therefore eliminated the possibility of reusing our display as a complete component. A comparable procedure appears in iFixit's Kindle 4 Screen Replacement guide, which describes full-surface bonding between the screen and bezel, removal of the screen in fragments, and subsequent clearing of residual adhesive and glass. The guide also identifies double-sided tape attaching the back cover [10]. Although it concerns a different Kindle model, it documents a similar constraint on disassembly.

Our testing did not establish whether the additional adhesive was necessary for the device's mechanical performance. It did establish a practical limitation: the screw connections were reversible, while separation of the bonded screen caused irreversible damage. The distinction matters because access to a component does not, by itself, make that component recoverable for reuse.

After separation, all of the adhesive residue we encountered was readily removed by light wiping with ethanol. Residue removal was therefore straightforward, even though separating the bonded components had been difficult. This suggests that solvent-assisted disassembly warrants further investigation, but our cleanup observation does not establish whether ethanol could have reached the bonded interfaces and released the screen before it fractured.

End-of-life implications

The iFixit guide demonstrates that a damaged screen can be replaced and the Kindle reassembled [10]. However, replacement and component reuse impose different requirements. A replacement procedure may tolerate destruction of an already damaged display; recovering a display for reuse requires preserving it during removal. In our teardown, adhesive bonding prevented that outcome and added a separation step after the fasteners had been removed.

The Kindle's end-of-life performance depends on both material selection and assembly design. Recyclable metals and potentially reusable components offer limited recovery benefits when adhesive bonds make their separation difficult or destructive.
